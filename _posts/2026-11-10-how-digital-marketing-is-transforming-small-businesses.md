A generation ago, a small shop or studio had limited options for reaching new customers: a sign, a newspaper ad, the local directory and word of mouth. Digital marketing has changed the maths. A bakery, a design studio or a one-person consultancy can now be found by people searching nearby, build a following that it owns, and measure what actually works, often for very little money.

<!--more-->

But the sheer number of channels can be overwhelming. Here's how the main ones work for small businesses, and where to start.

## Why digital levels the playing field

- **Customers search first.** Before visiting, calling or buying, many people look a business up online: opening hours, reviews, photos, prices.
- **Targeting replaces broadcasting.** Instead of paying to reach everyone, you can reach people actively looking for what you offer.
- **Everything is measurable.** You can see which posts, pages and emails bring enquiries, and stop spending on those that don't.
- **Small can be an advantage.** Personality, expertise and responsiveness often stand out more online than a big brand's polish.

## The core channels, in order of priority

### 1. Your local listing

For businesses serving a local area, a complete, accurate **Google Business Profile** (and the equivalents on other maps services) is often the single most valuable step. It is free. Add correct hours, categories, photos and services, and keep them up to date.

### 2. A simple, fast website

Social profiles are rented land, and your website is the one place you control. It doesn't need to be elaborate: clear information about what you do, where, for whom, and how to get in touch, on a site that loads quickly on a phone.

### 3. Search engine optimisation (SEO)

SEO means helping search engines understand and trust your site so it appears for relevant searches. Google's own *SEO Starter Guide* is a good, free place to begin. The fundamentals are:

- Give each page a clear, descriptive title and description.
- Write genuinely helpful content that answers the questions your customers ask. Google's guidance emphasises "people-first" content.
- Make sure the site works well on mobile and loads quickly.
- Earn mentions and links from relevant local organisations, suppliers and press.

Avoid anyone promising guaranteed rankings or instant results. Good SEO compounds slowly.

### 4. Reviews and reputation

Reviews influence decisions and are shown prominently in search and maps. Ask happy customers to leave one, respond politely to all reviews (including critical ones), and never buy fake reviews, which can breach platform rules and consumer protection law.

### 5. Email

An email list is an audience you own. A short, useful newsletter, with new products, seasonal advice or behind-the-scenes stories, keeps you in touch with people who already like you. Always get clear permission before adding someone and make unsubscribing easy.

### 6. Social media

Choose one or two platforms where your customers actually spend time, rather than trying to be everywhere. Visual businesses (food, fashion, interiors) often suit image- and video-led platforms. Professional services may do better on networks built around expertise. Consistency beats volume.

### 7. Paid advertising

Search and social ads can bring quick results but cost money every day they run. Start small, target narrowly, set a strict budget and measure enquiries or sales, not just clicks.

## A 30-day starter plan

1. **Week 1:** Claim and complete your business listings. Fix your website's contact details and opening hours.
2. **Week 2:** Rewrite your homepage and key service pages in plain language that answers customer questions.
3. **Week 3:** Ask ten recent customers for a review. Set up a simple email sign-up.
4. **Week 4:** Pick one social platform and plan two useful posts a week for the next month.

## Measure what matters

Track a few numbers that relate directly to revenue: enquiries, bookings, calls, sales and where they came from. Likes and followers are nice, but they don't pay rent.
