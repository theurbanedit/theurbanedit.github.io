Not long ago, working from home one day a week was a privilege negotiated with your manager. Today, remote and hybrid arrangements are a normal part of working life for many office-based professionals around the world, and whole industries, cities and even visa systems have adapted to them.

<!--more-->

## How we got here

Remote work did not appear overnight. Telecommuting was being discussed in the 1970s, and the spread of laptops, broadband, video calls and cloud software through the 2000s and 2010s made it increasingly practical. Freelancers, developers and distributed startups led the way, often working from home or from [coworking spaces]({{ '/how-coworking-spaces-are-changing-modern-work/' | relative_url }}).

The COVID-19 pandemic turned a slow trend into a mass experiment. In 2020, organisations that had never allowed remote work had to make it function within weeks. When offices reopened, many people did not want to go back full-time. Hybrid work, with a mix of office and remote days, became a common compromise.

Researchers have tracked this closely. The WFH Research project, run by economists including Nicholas Bloom, surveys how and where people work and has documented how working from home stabilised well above pre-pandemic levels.

## What the evidence says

The research is more nuanced than either enthusiasts or sceptics suggest.

- **Productivity can rise.** A widely cited randomised trial at a Chinese travel company, published in 2015, found call-centre employees working from home performed better than office-based colleagues, partly due to fewer breaks and quieter conditions.
- **But context matters.** Results vary by job, by worker and by how well a team is managed. Work that depends on mentoring, spontaneous collaboration or hands-on training can suffer when everyone is apart.
- **Workers value flexibility.** Many surveys find employees treat the option to work remotely as a meaningful benefit, and some say they would consider changing jobs to keep it.

## The rise of the digital nomad

Remote work also created a new kind of traveller: people who work for employers or clients in one country while living in another. Governments noticed. **Estonia** launched a Digital Nomad Visa in 2020, and a number of other countries, including **Croatia** and **Portugal**, have since introduced visas or permits aimed at remote workers.

The rules, including income thresholds, tax obligations, duration and health insurance, differ widely and change often. If you're considering it, check official government sources and get tax advice, as working from another country can affect both your tax position and your employer's.

## What it means for cities

Remote work is reshaping places as well as jobs:

- **City centres** that relied on daily office commuters have had to rethink, adding housing, culture and leisure alongside offices.
- **Smaller towns and suburbs** have attracted people who no longer need to live near the office.
- **"Third places"** such as cafés, libraries and coworking spaces have become informal workplaces for people who want to get out of the house.

## Making remote work work

For individuals, the basics matter most:

1. **Set up a proper workspace.** See [how to design a productive home workspace]({{ '/how-to-design-a-productive-home-workspace/' | relative_url }}).
2. **Over-communicate.** Share progress, ask questions openly, and write things down. Remote teams run on clarity.
3. **Protect boundaries.** Define your hours and stick to them, even when the laptop is always within reach.
4. **Invest in relationships.** Video coffee chats, team days and in-person meetups help counter isolation.
5. **Keep developing.** Ask actively for feedback and opportunities, which are easier to miss when you're not in the room.

For teams, the shift has encouraged clearer documentation, more deliberate meetings and greater attention to results rather than hours seen at a desk. Habits like those in [essential productivity habits for professionals]({{ '/essential-productivity-habits-for-professionals/' | relative_url }}) become even more important when nobody is looking over your shoulder.

## What comes next

The debate about how many office days are "right" will continue, and the answer will differ between companies, roles and people. What seems settled is that the old default, where everyone is in the same building five days a week, is no longer the only model. Work is increasingly something we do, not somewhere we go.
