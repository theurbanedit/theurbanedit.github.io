Lifestyle trends come and go at the speed of a social feed. Most fade within a season. But a few reflect deeper shifts in how people want to live, shaped by technology, work, cities and a growing interest in wellbeing.

<!--more-->

These are the trends we think are worth your attention, along with an easy way to try each.

## 1. Slow living

The slow movement grew out of the Slow Food campaign founded in Italy in the 1980s as a response to fast food. It has since spread to travel, fashion and everyday life. The idea isn't to do everything slowly. It's to do things at the *right* speed, with attention.

**Try it:** pick one daily activity, such as your morning coffee, a meal or your walk home, and do it without your phone.

## 2. Analogue hobbies

Film photography, vinyl records, paper journals, knitting, pottery and board games are popular partly *because* they're offline. They offer something screens rarely do: focus, tactile satisfaction and a finished object.

**Try it:** take a beginner's class in something you can make with your hands.

## 3. Digital boundaries

Rather than quitting technology, more people are setting rules for it: phone-free bedrooms, notification "quiet hours", grayscale screens, or app time limits. The goal is to use technology deliberately rather than by default.

**Try it:** charge your phone outside the bedroom for a week and notice the difference in your evenings and mornings.

## 4. Movement woven into the day

Not everyone loves the gym, and that's fine. The World Health Organization's guidance for adults is at least 150 to 300 minutes of moderate aerobic activity a week, plus muscle-strengthening activity on two or more days, and it notes that some activity is better than none. Walking meetings, cycling to work and taking the stairs all count.

**Try it:** add a 15-minute walk after lunch.

## 5. Walkable, local living

Interest is growing in neighbourhoods where daily needs (groceries, cafés, parks, schools, work) are within a short walk or bike ride. Planners often discuss this as the "15-minute city". For individuals, it often means rediscovering what's close to home.

**Try it:** for one weekend, do all your errands and leisure on foot or by bike.

## 6. Buying less, but better

Across fashion, homeware and tech, people are favouring fewer, higher-quality, longer-lasting purchases, often second-hand or repairable. It's good for budgets as well as the planet. Read more in [the future of sustainable fashion]({{ '/the-future-of-sustainable-fashion/' | relative_url }}).

**Try it:** before any non-essential purchase, wait 48 hours.

## 7. Homes that do more

With hybrid work, homes have become offices, gyms and studios as well. That has fuelled interest in calm, multi-purpose interiors. See [minimalist interior design ideas for modern homes]({{ '/minimalist-interior-design-ideas-for-modern-homes/' | relative_url }}) and [how to design a productive home workspace]({{ '/how-to-design-a-productive-home-workspace/' | relative_url }}).

**Try it:** clear one surface in your home and keep it clear for a month.

## 8. Flexible work, flexible lives

The [rise of remote work]({{ '/the-rise-of-remote-work-around-the-world/' | relative_url }}) has changed where people live and how they structure their days. Some use the freedom to travel, others to spend more time with family or move somewhere quieter.

**Try it:** if you work remotely, try a week working from a different neighbourhood, or a coworking space, to see how environment changes your energy.

## How to choose which trends to follow

Ask three questions before you adopt any new lifestyle habit:

1. **Does it solve a real problem in my life?**
2. **Can I try it cheaply, without buying a lot of new things?**
3. **Will I still want to do it in a year?**

A trend worth following should make your life simpler, healthier or more meaningful, not just more photogenic.
