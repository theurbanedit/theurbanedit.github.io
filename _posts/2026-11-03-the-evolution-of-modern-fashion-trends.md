Fashion has always changed, but how trends start, who sets them and how fast they move has transformed over the past century. Understanding that evolution helps explain why getting dressed today can feel both freer and more overwhelming than ever.

<!--more-->

## The age of the couturier

For much of the early twentieth century, trends flowed from the top down. A small number of Paris couture houses showed collections, and their silhouettes slowly filtered into department stores, dressmakers' patterns and magazines.

Designers could shift the shape of an era. Coco Chanel popularised relaxed jersey and simpler lines for women in the 1910s and 1920s. In 1947, Christian Dior's debut collection, quickly dubbed the "New Look", reintroduced nipped waists and full skirts after wartime austerity. Fashion historians still treat it as one of the most dramatic resets of the century.

## Ready-to-wear and youth culture

From the 1960s, the balance shifted. Ready-to-wear clothing made fashion more accessible, and young people, rather than couture clients, became a driving force. London's boutiques, the miniskirt, and later the music scenes of punk, hip-hop and grunge showed that trends could start on the street and move *up* into designer collections.

This "bubble-up" pattern still defines modern fashion: subcultures create a look, designers reinterpret it, and the high street spreads it.

## Fast fashion speeds everything up

From the late twentieth century, retailers developed supply chains that could take a design from sketch to shop in weeks rather than months. New styles arrived constantly, prices fell and the traditional two-season calendar blurred.

Fast fashion made trends accessible to almost everyone, but it also encouraged buying more and wearing less. The Ellen MacArthur Foundation's 2017 report on the textiles economy described how clothing production grew while the number of times garments were worn declined. ## Streetwear and the collapse of dress codes

Over recent decades, streetwear moved from skate shops and sneaker culture to the centre of luxury fashion. At the same time, offices relaxed their dress codes, and the [rise of remote work]({{ '/the-rise-of-remote-work-around-the-world/' | relative_url }}) further blurred the line between "work clothes" and everything else. Comfort, sportswear and versatile basics became mainstream style, not just weekend wear.

## The social-media trend cycle

Today, trends often begin online. A look can spread around the world within days through short videos and image feeds, gain a catchy name, and fade almost as quickly. Several things are different from the past:

- **Speed.** Micro-trends can rise and fall within a single season.
- **Fragmentation.** Instead of one dominant look, there are many coexisting aesthetics and niche communities.
- **Participation.** Anyone can set a trend, and brands often follow audiences rather than lead them.
- **Revival.** Earlier decades are constantly remixed, so "new" often means newly rediscovered.

## What this means for how we dress

When everything is a trend, nothing is. Many style-conscious people are responding by focusing on **personal style** rather than chasing every new look:

1. **Know your silhouettes.** Notice which shapes and proportions you feel best in, and build around them.
2. **Build a reliable core.** Well-made basics in colours you love make trend pieces easier to integrate.
3. **Try trends lightly.** Experiment through accessories, second-hand finds or a single piece rather than a whole new wardrobe.
4. **Choose longevity.** Ask whether you'll still want to wear something in three years.

## The next chapter

Fashion's future is likely to be shaped by the same forces that shaped its past: technology, culture, economics and the desire for self-expression. What's changing is the growing expectation that style should be thoughtful as well as exciting.
