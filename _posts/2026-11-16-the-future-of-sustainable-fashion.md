Sustainable fashion used to mean a small rail of organic cotton basics in the corner of a shop. Today it is one of the central questions facing the whole industry: how do you make clothing people love without the waste built into the current system?

<!--more-->

The answer won't come from a single fabric or a single brand. It is a shift in how clothes are designed, made, used and, crucially, what happens to them afterwards.

## Why the old model is under pressure

The fashion system most of us grew up with is *linear*: take raw materials, make garments, sell them, and throw them away. The Ellen MacArthur Foundation's 2017 report *A New Textiles Economy* set out how wasteful that is. It estimated that the equivalent of one garbage truck of textiles is landfilled or burned every second, and that much clothing is worn relatively few times before being discarded.

Faster trend cycles, lower prices and more frequent drops have pushed volumes up. Meanwhile regulators have started paying attention. The European Union's strategy for sustainable and circular textiles, for example, aims for products sold in the EU to be longer-lasting, repairable and recyclable.

## Five shifts shaping what comes next

### 1. Designing for a long life

The most sustainable garment is usually the one you already own. Designers are paying more attention to durability: stronger seams, better fabrics, classic cuts that outlast a season, and sizing that allows some adjustment. "Buy less, choose well" is an old idea, but it is becoming a design brief.

### 2. Circular materials

Brands are experimenting with recycled fibres, mono-material garments (one fibre type throughout, which makes recycling easier) and next-generation materials. Be wary of single-claim marketing, though. A recycled-polyester jacket worn three times is not more sustainable than a well-made wool coat worn for a decade.

### 3. Repair goes mainstream

Repair used to be something your grandparents did. Now mending services, brand-run repair programmes and visible-mending tutorials are making it normal, and even stylish, to keep clothes going.

### 4. Resale and rental

Second-hand platforms, vintage shops and rental services keep clothes in use for longer. For occasion wear in particular, renting can make far more sense than buying something you will wear once.

### 5. Transparency

Shoppers increasingly want to know where and how clothes are made. Look for brands that publish their supplier lists, explain their materials in specific terms and have their claims independently certified.

## How to spot greenwashing

Vague language is the biggest warning sign. Words like "eco", "conscious" or "green" mean little on their own. Ask instead:

- **What, specifically?** "Made with 100% recycled cotton" is a claim. "Planet-friendly" is a mood.
- **How much of the range?** A small "sustainable edit" alongside thousands of fast-fashion products tells you something.
- **Who verified it?** Recognised third-party certifications carry more weight than in-house labels.

## A practical wardrobe approach

You do not need to replace your wardrobe to dress more sustainably. In fact, replacing it would defeat the point. Try this instead:

1. **Audit what you own.** Note what you actually wear. The gaps are usually smaller than they seem.
2. **Adopt a "cost per wear" mindset.** A more expensive piece worn 100 times can cost less per wear than a cheap one worn twice.
3. **Care for things properly.** Wash less often, at lower temperatures, and air-dry where you can.
4. **Buy second-hand first** for categories like denim, outerwear and knitwear.
5. **Pass things on.** Sell, swap or donate pieces you no longer wear while they are still in good condition.

## The bigger picture

Sustainable fashion is also a cultural shift. Style is moving away from constant novelty towards personal signature: knowing what suits you and building on it.
