Search for "minimalist interiors" and you will find plenty of rooms that look beautiful and feel impossible to live in: bare white walls, a single chair and nowhere to put your keys. Real minimalism is more generous than that. It is about removing what distracts so that what remains, including light, materials, useful objects and the people in the room, can be enjoyed.

<!--more-->

Industrial designer Dieter Rams summed up his philosophy as "less, but better". It is a useful test for a home. The aim is not fewer things for their own sake, but better things that earn their place.

## Start by editing, not shopping

Minimalism begins with subtraction. Before buying a single storage box, go room by room and ask three questions about each object:

1. Do I use this regularly?
2. Does it make me happy to see it?
3. Would I buy it again today?

Anything that fails all three is a candidate to sell, donate or recycle. Work in small sessions, one drawer or shelf at a time, rather than trying to transform the whole house in a weekend.

## Ideas for a calm, modern minimalist home

### 1. Choose a restrained palette

Pick a base of two or three related colours, such as warm white, stone and charcoal, then add one accent you love. A limited palette makes a room feel coherent even when the objects in it are varied. Warm whites and soft greiges tend to feel more welcoming than stark, blue-toned whites.

### 2. Let materials do the decorating

When there are fewer objects, texture matters more. Oiled timber, linen, wool, lime-washed walls, stone and clay add depth without visual clutter. Natural materials also age well, which fits the "buy once" spirit of minimalism.

### 3. Hide the everyday, display the special

Closed storage is a minimalist's best friend. Built-in cupboards, handle-less fronts and storage benches keep the practical clutter of daily life out of sight. That leaves a few open shelves for things worth looking at: a ceramic bowl, a stack of books, a single plant.

### 4. Design with light

Keep window treatments simple so daylight can fill the room. Then layer artificial light: a soft overall light, task lighting where you read or cook, and one or two lamps for the evening. Warm-white bulbs make minimalist rooms feel inviting rather than clinical.

### 5. Give every room one focal point

A minimalist room still needs something to look at, whether that's a view, a large artwork, a sculptural light or a beautiful table. One strong focal point creates calm. Five competing ones create noise.

### 6. Bring nature in

The biologist E. O. Wilson popularised the term *biophilia* for our innate affinity with living things. Plants, natural light, views of greenery and natural materials are an easy way to soften minimalist spaces. Choose a few larger plants rather than many small ones.

### 7. Respect negative space

Empty space is not wasted space. A clear stretch of wall, an uncluttered countertop or a gap between furniture pieces gives the eye somewhere to rest and makes rooms feel bigger.

### 8. Prioritise comfort

A minimalist sofa should still be one you want to sink into. Rugs, cushions in tactile fabrics and a good throw make spare rooms liveable. If a design choice makes daily life harder, it is the wrong choice.

## Minimalism in small spaces

Minimalism is especially powerful in compact apartments:

- Choose furniture with legs to show more floor and make rooms feel larger.
- Use multi-purpose pieces, such as an extendable table, a bed with drawers or an ottoman with storage.
- Run storage to the ceiling to use vertical space.
- Keep the palette consistent between rooms so the home flows.

## Common minimalist mistakes

- **Buying "minimalist" décor** to fill the space you just cleared.
- **Removing all personality.** Family photos, travel finds and books belong in a minimalist home. Just curate them.
- **Forgetting acoustics.** Hard, bare rooms can echo. Rugs, curtains and upholstered furniture absorb sound.
