For most of the twentieth century, "going to work" meant going to *your* employer's building. Coworking quietly broke that link. A shared space, rented by the day or month, now sits somewhere between the corporate office and the kitchen table, and it has changed how millions of people think about where good work happens.

<!--more-->

This piece looks at where coworking came from, why it grew, and how to choose a space that actually helps you work better.

## From a single room to a global format

The word "coworking" is usually traced to San Francisco in 2005, when programmer Brad Neuberg opened a shared workspace for independent workers who wanted the structure and company of an office without the employer. The idea was modest: some desks, a schedule, and other people to talk to.

What made it spread was timing. Laptops and cloud software meant that a growing share of work could be done anywhere with Wi-Fi. Freelancing and small startups multiplied. Yet working alone at home turned out to have real costs, including isolation, blurred boundaries and few chances to meet people outside your own field.

Coworking answered those problems with something that already had a name. Sociologist Ray Oldenburg described cafés, barbershops and pubs as **"third places"**: informal public spaces, separate from home (the first place) and work (the second), where community forms. A good coworking space works the same way, except people also get work done there.

## What coworking actually changed

### Work became a service, not a lease

Traditional offices tie a business to multi-year leases, fit-out costs and fixed headcount. Coworking turned space into something you can subscribe to: a hot desk, a dedicated desk, a private room, or a meeting room by the hour. For a two-person startup or a consultant, that flexibility can be the difference between having a professional base and having none.

### Community became part of the product

The best operators learned that people don't only pay for the desk. They pay for the room around it: introductions, events, skill-sharing sessions and the chance conversation at the coffee machine. Many members find clients, collaborators or co-founders this way. That kind of value is hard to measure, and it is the main reason people stay.

### Companies started to rethink the headquarters

Large organisations noticed. Many now use flexible space for project teams, satellite offices close to where employees live, or overflow capacity, rather than committing to more permanent floors. Research on remote and hybrid work has fed the debate too. A widely cited 2015 randomised experiment by Nicholas Bloom and colleagues at a Chinese travel company found that call-centre staff working from home were more productive than their office-based peers. It also found that some workers missed the social side of the office and chose to return. Coworking sits in exactly that gap.

> The question is no longer "office or home?" but "which place suits this kind of work today?"

## The trade-offs nobody puts on the brochure

Coworking is not automatically better. Common frustrations include:

- **Noise and interruptions.** Open-plan rooms can be lively to the point of distraction. Look for phone booths and quiet zones.
- **Hidden costs.** Meeting-room credits, printing, guest passes and locker fees add up.
- **Privacy.** Client calls and confidential documents need a closed room, not a shared bench.
- **Community that is only marketing.** A busy events calendar is not the same as a welcoming culture.

## How to choose a coworking space

Before you sign up, book a day pass and use it as a real working day, not a tour. Then check:

1. **The commute.** A space you can reach in 15 minutes gets used. One that takes an hour gets abandoned.
2. **Work modes.** Is there somewhere for focused work, somewhere for calls and somewhere to meet? Our guide to [how creative workspaces encourage innovation]({{ '/how-creative-workspaces-encourage-innovation/' | relative_url }}) explains why that mix matters.
3. **Ergonomics.** Adjustable chairs, decent desk height and natural light matter more over a month than design-magazine furniture does.
4. **Connectivity.** Test the Wi-Fi at a busy time of day, and ask whether there is a wired option.
5. **The members.** Ask the community manager who works there. A room full of people in adjacent fields can be worth more than the location.
6. **The contract.** Check notice periods, what is included, and whether you can pause or downgrade.

## Where coworking goes next

Several trends are already visible. Spaces are spreading beyond city centres into suburbs and smaller towns, closer to where people live. Hotels, libraries and cafés are adding work-friendly zones. Operators are also specialising, with spaces built for creatives, scientists, parents with on-site childcare, or particular industries.

What stays constant is the insight that started it all: people do their best work when they can choose their environment, and most of us work better with other people around.
